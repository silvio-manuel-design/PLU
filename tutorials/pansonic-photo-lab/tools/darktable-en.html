darktable

Professional open-source RAW workflow and image processing editor for digital photographers — free, cross-platform, and non-destructive.

What is darktable?

darktable is a professional RAW workflow and image processing editor for digital photographers. It was developed from the ground up to cover the entire RAW development process — from importing camera files through editing to exporting in various target formats. The software is fully open source under the GPL 3.0 license and is continuously developed by an active, worldwide community.

darktable is available for Windows, macOS, and Linux and offers a professional workflow that can compete with commercial alternatives like Adobe Lightroom in many areas. Photographically savvy users particularly appreciate the modular pipeline, which allows precise control over every editing step, as well as the integrated color management for calibrated workflows.

The philosophy of darktable is based on non-destructive editing: your original RAW always remains unchanged. All changes are stored as a history log and can be undone, adjusted, or completely removed at any time. This gives you maximum flexibility and security for your valuable shots.

Philosophy

darktable follows the philosophy of non-destructive editing: your original RAW always remains unchanged. All changes are stored as a history log and can be undone or adjusted at any time. This means you can experiment without fear of permanent mistakes — the original state is always recoverable.

Highlights & Features

darktable offers an impressive collection of features otherwise found only in expensive commercial programs:

Non-destructive editing

All changes are stored as metadata; the original RAW remains untouched. You can jump back to any editing step at any time and maintain multiple versions of an image in parallel.

Modular pipeline

Over 60 editing modules can be applied in any order and combination — from basic corrections through detail enhancement to creative color effects and film emulations.

Professional color management

Full support for ICC profiles, various color spaces, and calibrated monitors for precise color reproduction throughout the entire workflow.

Lua scripting

Automate recurring tasks and create your own extensions with the integrated Lua interface. The community offers numerous scripts for advanced workflows.

Lighttable & Darkroom

Two main workspaces: the Lighttable for sorting, rating, and comparison; the Darkroom for detailed development of individual images.

Powerful export

Export in all common formats with variable resolution, watermark embedding, metadata preservation, and batch processing for large projects.

Interface and Workspaces

darktable is divided into several specialized views that map the classic workflow of a photographer — from selection to completion:

Lighttable

The digital darkroom: here you import, sort, rate, and compare your images. The Lighttable offers filters, collections, and star ratings for efficient image management. You can compare images, reject shots, and organize them in folder structures.

Darkroom

The editing room: here you develop your RAW files with over 60 modules. The modular pipeline allows precise control over every aspect of the image — from exposure to creative color design.

Map

Geotagging and map view: assign locations to your shots and browse them geographically. Supports GPX tracks and reverse geocoding for spatial organization of your archive.

Tethering

Direct shooting from the computer: control your camera from the computer and import images automatically — ideal for studio and product photography where immediate image control is important.

The darktable Workflow Step by Step

A typical workflow in darktable includes these steps — from shooting to finished image. This structure helps you work systematically and get the best out of your RAW files:

01

Import & Organization

Import your RAW files into the Lighttable. Create collections, assign star ratings from 1-5, and add tags to structure your images. The filter function helps you quickly find the best ones. Use color labels to mark images by editing status.

02

Basic Corrections in the Darkroom

Switch to the Darkroom and start with basic adjustments: exposure, white balance, contrast, and saturation. The Exposure module and White Balance are your starting points. Here you lay the foundation for all further editing.

03

Detail Optimization

Work on sharpness, noise reduction, and details. The Sharpen module, Denoise (Profiled) with camera-specific profiles, and local contrast enhancement bring out the maximum from your sensor — without looking artificial.

04

Color & Look

Use Color Balance RGB, Color Zones, or the Filmic RGB module for creative color design. Create styles that you can apply to any number of images to maintain a consistent look across entire series.

05

Local Adjustments & Retouching

Use masks, gradient filters, and the Retouch module to work selectively. Parametric masks allow precise selections based on brightness, color, or hue — for pinpoint corrections.

06

Export & Publication

Export to JPEG, TIFF, PNG, or WebP. darktable's export module offers resolution control, watermarks, metadata embedding, and batch processing for large projects. Create different export presets for web, print, and archive.

Pro Tips for darktable

Master keyboard shortcuts

Learn the most important keyboard shortcuts to significantly speed up your workflow: Ctrl+E for export, Ctrl+Z to undo, Space for before/after comparison. Lighttable navigation works with arrow keys, and you assign star ratings with the number keys 1-5. In the Darkroom, you can use Tab to toggle the side panels on and off for more image space. D opens the Darkroom directly from the Lighttable.

Use styles and presets

Create your own styles for recurring looks: develop an image to your liking, save the settings as a style, and apply it to entire series with one click. This is especially useful for wedding reports, portrait sessions, or landscape series where consistency is important. You can also import and export styles — the community offers thousands of free presets that you can load directly into darktable. Experiment with different looks to find your own style.

File Formats & Compatibility

Understanding the different file formats is crucial for a professional workflow. Each format has its strengths and weaknesses — here is an overview:

RAW

Capture — Maximum flexibility — Large files

TIFF

Archive/Print — Lossless, 16-bit — Very large files

JPEG

Web/Social — Small, compatible — Compression artifacts

PNG

Web/Graphics — Lossless, transparency — Larger than JPEG

WebP

Modern web — Small, good quality — Not everywhere supported

DNG

Standard RAW — Manufacturer-independent — Can be larger

For the professional workflow we recommend: always shoot in RAW format, develop in 16-bit TIFF for archive storage, and export JPEGs in various sizes for web, social media, and print.

Best Practices for the Workflow

A well-thought-out workflow saves time and guarantees consistent results. Here are proven methods that professional photographers apply daily:

Consistent folder structure

Create a separate folder for each project with subfolders for RAW, editing, and export. This way you keep track even in large projects.

Date-based naming

Use the format YYYY-MM-DD for folders and files. Chronological sorting is the simplest form of organization.

Rate immediately after import

Go through new images right away and assign stars. This way you know later which shots are worth editing.

Backup strategy

Keep RAW files in at least two places. External hard drives and cloud storage complement each other ideally for redundant security.

Develop your own style

Experiment with different looks, but aim for a recognizable style in the long term. This makes your work unmistakable.

Calibrated monitor

A calibrated screen is the basis for precise color judgment. Without calibration you are working in the dark — no matter how good your software is.

Open-source photo software has developed rapidly in recent years. What began as a niche for tech enthusiasts is now a serious alternative to commercial products:

The first versions of today's programs appeared in the early 2000s. Driven by communities of passionate developers and photographers, features were expanded step by step. Troubleshooting & Problem Solving

Even the best software can sometimes behave unexpectedly. Here are the most common problems and their solutions so you can quickly get back to productive work:

Crashes on Startup

Delete the configuration cache and restart the program. Make sure your graphics driver is up to date. Disable hardware acceleration if the problem persists.

RAW Files Not Readable

Check if your camera is already supported. Update the software to the latest version. Convert to DNG with Adobe DNG Converter as a workaround if needed.

Colors Look Wrong

Calibrate your monitor and ensure the correct color profile is set. Check the color management settings in the software for ICC profile support.

Slow Performance

Reduce preview resolution, close other programs, and increase the memory cache in settings. An SSD significantly speeds up file access.

Color Management & Profiles

Accurate colors are the foundation of any professional image editing. Without proper color management, you risk your images looking completely different on other displays or in print than intended:

sRGB

Web, Social Media — Standard for all online content

Adobe RGB

Print, Archive — When you print professionally

ProPhoto RGB

RAW editing — For maximum color space retention

CMYK

Offset printing — Only when requested by print shop

Monitor ICC

Display — Always — for calibrated viewing

Our tip: Work in a large color space (Adobe RGB or ProPhoto) and only convert to the target format on export. This way you retain maximum flexibility for all output paths. A calibrated monitor is essential — invest in a pro display or calibration device.

Export Profiles & Automation

Manually exporting every single file is time-consuming and error-prone. With export profiles and batch processing, you automate recurring tasks:

Web Optimization

JPEG, 80% quality, sRGB, 1920px width, 72 DPI. For fast loading times and compatible appearance on all devices. Ideal for portfolio websites and blog posts.

Print Profile

TIFF, 300 DPI, Adobe RGB, uncompressed. For professional gallery-quality prints. Talk to your print shop about specific requirements and ICC profiles.

Social Media

JPEG, 85% quality, sRGB, 2048px width. Optimized for Instagram, Facebook, and similar. Consider the different aspect ratios of each platform.

Archive Profile

TIFF, 16-bit, uncompressed, embedded metadata. Maximum quality for long-term archiving. Store on redundant media and document your workflow steps.

Performance Optimization

Smooth working requires a well-configured environment. Here are measures that noticeably speed up your workflow:

RAM

At least 16 GB RAM is recommended for professional RAW editing. For large files or panoramas, 32 GB makes sense.

SSD for System & Cache

Install the software and place the cache on a fast NVMe SSD. The difference is night and day.

Multi-Core Usage

Enable the use of all CPU cores in settings. Export and rendering benefit massively from parallel processing.

GPU Acceleration

Use OpenCL or CUDA if available. Modern graphics cards significantly speed up preview and export.

Regular Cache Cleanup

Old cache files consume storage space and can affect performance. Clear the cache monthly.

Split Catalog

With thousands of images, it makes sense to divide the catalog into thematic or temporal units.

Hardware Recommendations

The right hardware is the foundation for a smooth workflow. Here are recommendations for different budgets and requirements:

Beginner

6-core CPU, 16 GB RAM, 512 GB SSD, 24" Full HD monitor with IPS panel. Sufficient for hobby photographers and small projects.

Advanced

8-core CPU, 32 GB RAM, 1 TB NVMe SSD, 27" 4K monitor with 100% sRGB coverage. Ideal for semi-professional work and high-resolution RAW files.

Pro

12+ cores, 64 GB RAM, 2 TB NVMe SSD, dedicated graphics card, 32" 4K HDR monitor with Adobe RGB. For full-time photographers and large projects.

More important than the absolute latest hardware is a balanced configuration. A fast processor brings little if the RAM becomes the bottleneck. Invest first in RAM and SSD, then in CPU and monitor.

Workspace Setup

An ergonomic workspace saves your eyes and back during long editing sessions. Pay attention to these aspects:

Lighting

Use indirect light without direct reflections on the monitor. A daylight lamp beside the desk improves color perception.

Monitor Position

The top of the screen should be at eye level. The ideal distance is 60-70 cm. Tilt the monitor slightly backward.

Chair & Desk Height

Your elbows form a 90-degree angle. Feet are flat on the floor. An adjustable desk is the best investment.

Surroundings Color

Gray or neutral wall colors prevent color reflections. Avoid intense colors in the immediate vicinity of the monitor.

Input Devices

A graphics tablet enables more precise editing than a mouse. For retouching and masking it is practically indispensable.

Take Breaks

The 20-20-20 rule: Every 20 minutes look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. This rests the eyes and preserves your color perception.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Here we answer the questions most frequently asked by participants:

Do I need to master all tools?

No. Focus first on one program that fits your workflow. Many professional photographers use only a single tool for 90% of their work. Deep mastery of one program is more valuable than superficial knowledge of many.

Are open-source programs really free?

Yes, the software itself is free. You can download, install, and use it without restrictions. Some projects welcome donations, but that is voluntary. You get professional software without monthly subscription costs.

Is Lightroom worth it for beginners?

Lightroom Mobile is a good entry option since the basic version is free. For desktop, however, we recommend open-source alternatives because they incur no monthly costs and you keep your files locally.

Which file format should I use?

Always shoot in your camera's RAW format. This gives you maximum flexibility in editing. For final export, use JPEG for web and TIFF for high-quality prints or archiving.
