The 180-Degree Rule

Stand with your back to the window. Natural light illuminates your subject evenly — no expensive equipment needed. With artificial light: A single lamp from the side creates more depth than frontal lighting.

Your Photo Location

Photos don't happen in a vacuum — they happen somewhere. Whether you're setting up a still life in your room, shooting a street reportage downtown, or photographing a sunset in the park: Every location has its own rules, challenges, and possibilities. Outdoor Pro Tip

The "golden hour" (1 hour after sunrise / before sunset) transforms any location into a photo paradise. In midday sun: find shade or use the hard shadows for dramatic effects.

The Hybrid Setup
Indoor + Outdoor = maximum flexibility

The best photographers master both worlds. A portrait indoors, the location outdoors. A product at home, the model in nature. The hybrid setup is pro mode — and it's easier than you think.

The golden rule: Indoor for controlled light and calm composition. Outdoor for atmosphere, dynamics, and natural backgrounds. Combine both in one shoot and your photos immediately gain professional depth.

Hybrid Workflow

Start with a controlled indoor setup for your main subject. Then find an outdoor spot for environmental shots. Combine both in editing — the result looks like an editorial shoot.

Practice Makes Perfect

Theory is important — but you only learn photography by doing. Your first photo won't be perfect. That's the plan. Every pro started with bad pictures.

Exercise A: Indoor — The 5-Object Still Life

  1. 1
    Set Up Studio
    Window to the side, neutral background, tripod — your controlled space
  2. 2
    Collect 5 Textures
    Glass, wood, fabric, metal, plant — everyday objects with different surfaces
  3. 3
    Arrange
    Arrange them so they don't overlap — each object must be visible
  4. 4
    Shoot 10 Photos
    Different angles: from above, diagonal, eye level — experiment freely
  5. 5
    Analyze
    Check on the display: Which angle shows the textures best?

Goal: Control over light and composition. A good still life is 70% setup and 30% shutter press.

Exercise B: Outdoor — The 20-Minute Walk

  1. 1
    Travel Light
    Leave your home. Take only your smartphone — nothing else.
  2. 2
    10 Minutes Out
    Walk in one direction. Pay attention to: light, shadows, textures, colors
  3. 3
    20 Snapshots
    At least 20 photos — no zoom, no filters, no overthinking
  4. 4
    10 Conscious Photos
    Turn around and walk back. This time consciously slower — every click counts
  5. 5
    Compare & Learn
    Which photos feel more authentic? Which are technically better? What did you learn?

Goal: See the world with photographic eyes. Outside you learn light, movement, and timing — the three pillars of visual storytelling.

Remember

Every photo you delete taught you something. The camera doesn't like perfectionists — it likes those who keep pressing the shutter.

Eye Level
Natural Perspective

Eye level is the most important factor for a natural photo. Shooting from above or below distorts your subject's proportions. With people, this quickly looks dominant (from above) or submissive (from below).

For portraits: Lens at the model's eye height.
For children: Kneel down or sit.
For animals: Lie on the ground if necessary.
For architecture: Eye level = 1.60m above ground.

Pro Tips:
  • For animals: Get on eye level — suddenly you're in their world
  • For selfies: Hold the smartphone slightly above eye level, not far above
  • Experiment with low angles for dramatic effects
Stability
The Professional Stance

Camera shake is the most common cause of blurry photos. Even in daylight, an unsteady hand can ruin an image. The right body posture is your free tripod.

Joby GorillaPod — flexible legs, clamps anywhere
Neewer Table Tripod — €20, solid quality
Manfrotto Compact — professional, but pricier
DIY — stack of books, bag as counterweight

Pro Tips:
  • Lean against a wall — reduces camera shake by 80%
  • Use the self-timer (3s) to avoid vibrations from tapping
  • For landscapes: Tripod on solid ground, not on wooden decks
Breath Control
The Sniper Technique

Every breath moves your body — and therefore the camera. Pro photographers use a technique borrowed from snipers: Exhale, pause briefly, press gently.

It sounds simple, but the difference is enormous. In long exposures or low light, a steady hand can make the difference between "blurry" and "sharp as a razor."

Pro Tips:
  • Count silently: 1 (inhale) — 2 (exhale) — 3 (hold) — click
  • Take 2–3 deep breaths before releasing the shutter
  • Press the shutter gently, not abruptly
Declutter
Less Is More

A busy background draws attention away from the main subject. The human eye automatically seeks contrast — and a colorful background always wins against a subtle subject.

The difference between an amateur photo and a pro photo often lies not in the subject, but in what's happening around it. Less distraction = more attention on what matters.

Pro Tips:
  • Move 2 steps left or right — often that's enough
  • Use a wide aperture (f/1.8–f/2.8) for soft background
  • Position the subject so the background is far away
Light Direction
The 180-Degree Rule

Light is the heart of every photograph. The direction from which light comes determines mood, depth, and drama. The simplest rule: Stand with your back to the light source.

Window light — soft, natural, perfect for portraits
Cloudy sky — natural diffusion light outdoors
White wall / cardboard — as reflector for indirect light

Pro Tips:
  • The "Golden Hour" (1h after sunrise / before sunset) is magical
  • Use a white cardboard as reflector to fill harsh shadows
  • In strong backlight: Use HDR mode or expose for the subject
Test Shot
The Pro Check

A test shot costs nothing — but a missed moment is gone forever. Pros always take a test shot before capturing the "real" image. It's their insurance against technical mistakes.

The test shot shows you immediately if focus, exposure, and composition are right. A quick glance at the display is enough to spot problems before the decisive moment is gone.

Pro Tips:
  • Zoom into the display and check the eyes — are they sharp?
  • Check exposure: Too bright? Too dark? Details in highlights?
  • White balance: Do colors look natural?
